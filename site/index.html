How it works

There are three different truths about a website.

Most privacy tools tell you one of them. The interesting part is the gap between them.

01 — Declared

What the site says

Veyl fetches the privacy policy and, where one exists, the separate cookie policy — your browser does the fetching, and they are read on your device. Out comes a set of checkable claims, each keeping the sentence it came from.

02 — Observed

What the site does

Every company the page contacts, every cookie and its lifetime, what is kept in browser storage, and which browser APIs associated with fingerprinting were called — and whether any of it happened before you were asked.

03 — Compared

Where they differ

A policy promising only strictly necessary cookies before consent, next to the seven marketing services that had already loaded. Quoted from the site, set against what your browser actually did.

The policy said

“We use only strictly necessary cookies before you consent to our use of cookies and similar technologies.”

Your browser did

  • Google Ads & Ad Manager
  • Meta Pixel
  • Hotjar — session recording
  • Criteo
  • TikTok Pixel
Possible discrepancy

All contacted before any choice was made. Veyl shows you the sentence and the evidence side by side, and leaves the conclusion to you.

What it won't say

No score out of a hundred.

A number like 62/100 looks authoritative and hides how much of the picture was actually visible. Veyl reports a level per dimension, and says separately how confident it is. Two of those levels are doing careful work.

NONE SEEN

Veyl was watching and saw nothing. Not the same as proving nothing happened.

LOW

A little measurement, and nothing that follows you between websites.

MEDIUM

The site learns more about you than it needs in order to work.

HIGH

Your visit is reported to companies whose business is following people.

UNKNOWN

Veyl could not look, or cannot establish it. Not a quiet way of saying “low”.

The line Veyl will not cross

It will tell you that advertising trackers loaded, and that the policy permits sharing with advertising partners. It will not tell you the site sold your data.

That turns on contracts no browser extension can see. So Veyl says so, and puts the question on a permanent list of things it cannot answer.

The awkward question

What does the privacy tool do with your data?

Nothing leaves this device. Not as a promise — as a property of the build. There is one outbound request in the whole extension, and it fetches the policy of the site you are already on.

What it does

  • Asks for no website access when you install it — you allow one site at a time
  • Holds a visit in memory and destroys it when the tab closes
  • Blocks advertising and session recording, never sign-in, payments or bot protection
  • Answers questions using Chrome's on-device model, where your browser provides one
  • Publishes its scoring method, tracker data and privacy boundary for anyone to check

What it never does

  • Send your browsing anywhere — there is no server to send it to
  • Keep a list of the sites you have visited, or hashes of them
  • Record a cookie's value; the evidence has no field for one
  • Contain analytics, crash reporting or telemetry of any kind
  • Ask you to make an account
